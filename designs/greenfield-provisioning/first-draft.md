# Provisioning can answer before it reads the cluster

**TL;DR.** Karpenter's provisioning loop simulates pending pods against every node in the cluster. On a large cluster the simulation can take minutes. This RFC proposes computing a feasible answer first and spending a bounded budget to improve it via simulation. The feasible answer is what we would launch for the pending pods if the cluster were empty: one greenfield NodeClaim per planned node. We precompute affinity and topology information from cluster state; after that, we can build one or more greenfield NodeClaims for any set of pending pods without an expensive simulation. The interface is one setting, an improvement budget for the search: unset, the default, keeps today's full simulation; zero skips the search entirely; anything between caps its time. The search replaces greedy per-pod placement and discards any answer costlier than the greenfield NodeClaims.

## The problem

Provisioning simulates each batch of pending pods against existing nodes, inflight nodes, and candidate new nodes. Each pending pod is checked for fit against existing nodes first, and the walk ends early only on a successful fit, so the pods that need new capacity are checked against every node, every cycle. One report from a large-cluster fork says removing ready nodes from the simulation cut provisioning from minutes to milliseconds. An existing node has concrete scheduling details and cannot be further constrained, so each check against one is cheap. A cheap check repeated across every pod and node still costs pods times nodes per cycle. When the team has investigated this in the past, the gains have felt small, plausibly because they are big in big clusters, small in medium clusters, and not noticeable at all in small clusters. The POC tested this. Where pending pods need new capacity, simulation time grows linearly in existing nodes while greenfield construction stays flat: the speedup is 1.0-1.2x at 100 nodes, 1.3-2.1x at 1,000, 2.4-4.8x at 5,000, and 3.7-11x at 10,000, exactly the shape above. Where pending pods fit existing capacity, the simulation early-exits per pod and beats greenfield construction by up to 10x, so the greenfield answer pays off exactly where pods need new capacity. Controls and the full matrix are in perf-results.md.

Ready nodes are in the simulation today for a good reason. We can only guess if the kube-scheduler has considered binding a pod to a node, and that guess can become out of date quickly. Simulating ready nodes hedges against acting on a bad guess, but the simulation scales poorly and we can use the greenfield NodeClaim to prune the simulation space, branch-and-bound style.

## Greenfield NodeClaims

A greenfield NodeClaim is an ordinary NodeClaim, computed from five inputs: (1) the pods' requests and constraints, (2) the NodePool specs, (3) current prices and instance availability, (4) the per-NodePool daemonset footprint, and (5) pod counts by topology domain. For input (4), daemonsets remove capacity from candidate instances. For input (5), the counts are kept per spread constraint and per pod affinity and anti-affinity term, required or preferred, including the anti-affinity terms of running pods. Each of those constraints reduces to the same question: which domains contain matching pods. For zone-scoped keys, a handful of counts is enough. For node-scoped keys that would mean one count per node, and whether that can be compressed is an open question. The skew check compares a fresh domain against the minimum, which suggests storing one number, but required affinity and anti-affinity at hostname scope read individual host counts. The POC kept the per-hostname map; any single-number scheme needs its own equivalence argument. The counts are maintained by watching node labels and pod locations, and the POC measured both regimes: applying one pod or node event costs 2-6 microseconds regardless of cluster size, while re-deriving the counts from a snapshot costs about 80 milliseconds per batch at 1,000 nodes and 800 milliseconds at 10,000, growing linearly (derivation-results.md). The watcher is cheap enough to run continuously; snapshot re-derivation is the recovery path. However big the cluster, the zone-scoped counts stay small, and the computation runs the same, empty cluster or giant one.

Between snapshot and launch, two inputs are likely to change: instance availability and the domain counts. If an offering becomes unavailable after we choose it, the launch fails, and we retry with the next offering. When a pod's constraints depend on the counts, a spread constraint or a pod (anti-)affinity term, its claim can go stale, and the execute-time check below catches it; we call these pods coupled. Preferred terms do not couple, because a preference never invalidates a claim. The builder strips pod-count-reading preferred terms before solving and builds the claim preference-blind; the kube-scheduler applies the preferences when it binds pods onto the launched capacity. A preference influences neither a claim's validity nor the execute-time check. Any other pod is uncoupled. Validity, though, is a property of the whole claim: every pod packed onto a claim shares its fate, so a claim holding both coupled and uncoupled pods is coupled, and on failure all its pods are regenerated together. What stays valid as the cluster changes is a claim whose pods are all uncoupled. When packing routes an uncoupled pod onto a coupled claim, the pod still holds a valid fallback, its own greenfield claim recomputed without the coupled pods, used when the shared claim fails its check. Some pods get no claim at all: hostname-scope affinity pins a pod to one existing node, an allocated device pins it to its hardware, and sometimes nothing the NodePool offers fits. Those pods stay on the current path, today's full simulation.

The total cost of the greenfield NodeClaims is the acceptance threshold: phase two actuates a different answer only when it costs less, otherwise we launch the greenfield claims. The threshold is enforced by construction, and it has to be, because it is not a property of today's search: under attributed pricing, greedy per-pod placement produced an answer costlier than the greenfield claims in ninety-five of two hundred generated scenarios, at a median of 49 percent over. Even priced on new claims alone, where existing capacity looks free, greedy still exceeded the incumbent once through a packing anomaly. On an empty cluster the two computations solve the same problem and the greenfield claims never cost more. With existing capacity, acceptance prices a candidate as its new spend plus the net reclamation it forecloses. Parking a pod onto an existing node converts waste into utility: the node is already paid for, its idle share is waste, and the placement occupies that waste at zero new spend. The only cost parking can carry is the consolidation savings it takes off the table. Those savings count only when the node's own consolidation policy would have realized them: a node blocked by a do-not-disrupt pod or a blocking PDB has none to lose, a WhenEmpty pool reclaims only empty nodes, and a Balanced pool approves a shrink through its own scoring predicate, which implies a disruption price of pool cost over k times pool disruption (BalancedK). That implied price is a spot rate stretched over the horizon until consolidation would have acted. A placement that reads the pool totals is coupled in this RFC's sense, the execute-time check reprices it with fresh totals at commit, and the drift beyond commit is a forecast we name as one. For a fixed batch, utility is the same wherever the pods land, so minimizing total cost and minimizing total waste coincide; candidate answers differ only in timing and churn. Attribution (pricing_paper.md, a companion to this document) stays as the billing model and the waste-visibility signal: the pod pays its alone_cost times f, where f is the node price divided by the sum of alone_costs on the node, so well-packed nodes bill at a discount and wasteful nodes at a surcharge. A bill prices waste while it stands; acceptance prices what a placement changes. In the worked example, a 3.60 node holds one small pod and a matching batch pod parks there: attribution bills the parking at 1.80 against a 1.00 incumbent, consolidation then shrinks both pods onto a 1.00 replacement, and the parking was optimal by end of day, so acceptance takes it.

## The architecture

We propose splitting provisioning into two phases inside the existing batch window.

**Phase one: build the greenfield NodeClaims for the batch.** The work depends on the batch alone, and the computation is largely stateless: of its inputs, only the counts and the daemonset footprint come from the cluster, and only the counts change quickly. The result is valid to actuate as-is, and its cost is the number every other answer must beat, so we call it the incumbent.

**Phase two: try to beat it.** The incumbent is only a starting point. Phase two searches for cheaper answers: stack pods onto open claims from prior batches, place pods onto ready nodes, split a claim into smaller ones, merge claims, re-pack pods across claims. Phase two also generates the candidate moves for coupled pods, and a candidate must satisfy the counts before it is priced. Any candidate must beat the incumbent's cost, so the search discards weak candidates early. The improvement budget, a cap on the time this search may spend, bounds it; a name like improvementBudgetSeconds is open for review, and unset stays the default so nothing changes for anyone who does nothing. When the budget runs out, we actuate the best answer in hand. On a small cluster the search completes and finds whatever cheap improvements exist. On a cluster where the simulation is slow, we are unlikely to find a solution that beats the incumbent, and the budget keeps us from wasting too much time.

**Check, commit, execute.** Any decision that read shared state can go stale between the snapshot and the launch: a ready node fills, or the domain counts change. We check each placement immediately before committing; any earlier and the check itself can go stale. What passes is committed and executed. What fails is regenerated: for an uncoupled pod we fall back to its greenfield NodeClaim, which read no shared state, and for a coupled pod we recompute against fresh counts, the same computation performed in phase one.

The design separates three concerns. Generation produces claims and candidate placements, and is a pure function of its inputs: the five above plus Karpenter's own open claims. Planning selects what to actuate against shared budgets. NodePool limits are used here, because two claims that fit alone can jointly exceed a limit. The bound is soft. Launches can partially fail, so the planner sizes what it attempts this cycle, and the next cycle re-plans from what actually launched. A claim under a spent limit is valid and unaffordable this cycle, and the planner holds it rather than the generator rejecting it. Actuation checks whatever depended on shared state and falls back when needed.

## The budget makes the simulation time a choice

Today every cluster runs the full simulation, however long it takes; the time budget makes that a choice. Skipping ready nodes assumes pending pods need new capacity. Provisioning and consolidation both attempt to minimize the free capacity available. With a short time budget, the search can miss a placement that running or launching capacity could have held, and we launch a node we did not need. With more time it misses fewer, and every batch waits up to the budget.

When people want the fast answer, they get the fast answer. A fast answer is probably worth paying for, especially in big clusters where provisioning can take multiple minutes. Extra nodes appear in node counts and the bill, waiting appears in provisioning latency, and an operator can change the setting and watch those two numbers.

## The POC

Phase one is implemented against upstream Karpenter on the greenfield-provisioning-poc branch: the classifier, the domain counts, the daemonset footprint, and a builder that runs the existing scheduler with no existing nodes. Correctness rests on two oracles: a replay through Karpenter's own simulation against reconstructed cluster state, and the real kube-scheduler binding pods against the envtest apiserver. Property tests assert the cost threshold where it is a theorem and measure it where it is not; across two hundred generated occupied-cluster scenarios, the three prices of one simulation answer come apart on schedule: the marginal price accepts 168 of 200 at a median gap of 95 percent under the incumbent, attribution accepts 98 at a median of parity, and the acceptance price, new spend plus foreclosed net reclamation, accepts 146 at a median of 31 percent under at gross and 140 under the Balanced policy's implied rate. A second flavor gives about half the occupancy nodes a do-not-disrupt running pod; on the 127 scenarios that park pods onto blocked nodes, attribution accepts 61 and the acceptance price accepts 114, every divergence in the direction the parking argument predicts. Halving or doubling the Balanced implied rate flips at most 14 of 200 decisions per flavor, so the spot-rate forecast carries little of the result. An incremental count tracker, property-checked for equivalence against fresh derivation, supplied the maintenance numbers. pkg/greenfield/README.md on the branch has the commands that reproduce every number in this document; the full matrices are in perf-results.md and derivation-results.md.

## Future work

Three follow-ups are worth pursuing separately, and they share a shape: each replaces a fixed rule with evidence about where search effort pays. The kube-scheduler marks pods it has rejected with an Unschedulable condition. The mark is evidence that no existing node fit at some past moment, so phase two can bias its time toward unmarked pods. The mark can be stale, and the execute-time check already covers that. Provisioning for the whole batch stays immediate either way, since waiting for the scheduler would add its lag to ours. Launch success under insufficient-capacity errors is a probability we can estimate rather than a bit to cache, and a companion document proposes replacing the ICE cache with a belief over offerings; this RFC treats launch failure as a retry with the next offering. The time budget can also be set adaptively: node cost and provisioning latency are both observable, so a controller can adjust it from those two signals instead of an operator picking a number.
